Porto Ercole is one small harbour wearing three Spanish forts, which is a lot of masonry per fishing boat, and tells you what the Spaniards thought of their neighbours back when they ran this coast as the Presidi. The forts stand over the town at exactly the angle their engineers chose to discourage visitors, and we went up anyway, since discouraging visitors is all they do for a living now.

::prompt Which fort got climbed and which got skipped?

Below all that defence the water carried on in the usual Argentario manner, a blue that makes the hardship reporting difficult to file with a straight face, and there was a beach involved, which is the whole problem with this stretch of Italy.

::prompt Any swim, or was the beach the view?

::doctrine Conditions remain grim.

Somewhere in the middle of the day there was lunch, because there always is, though the paperwork on it is thin.

::prompt What was lunch and who found it?

The reason Porto Ercole is in this book at all is that this is where Caravaggio stopped. July 1610, the pardon finally arranged, and the pardon and the paintings both on a felucca that had sailed without him, so he chased it, up this coast, on foot, in that month, in that heat. He was thirty-eight. He got exactly as far as Porto Ercole, and on the 18th of July he was done, which the felucca does not appear to have noticed.

::drop A man running out of coastline, the boat holding its line. Was he chasing the paintings or the pardon?

So the town kept the grave, and it is up there somewhere still, filed under a name nobody could spell. The paintings kept the water. That part of the story was not finished at Porto Ercole, and neither are we with it.
