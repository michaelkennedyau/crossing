::map porto-ercole

Three Spanish forts, one fishing harbour. That is a lot of masonry per boat.

This coast was the Presidi once, and the forts tell you what the Spaniards thought of their neighbours. They still stand over the town at exactly the angle their engineers chose to discourage visitors. We went up anyway. Discouraging visitors is all they do for a living now.

::prompt Which fort got climbed and which got skipped?

Below all that defence: the usual Argentario blue. The kind that makes hardship reporting difficult to file with a straight face. There was also a beach, which is the whole problem with this stretch of Italy.

::prompt Any swim, or was the beach the view?

::doctrine Conditions remain grim.

Somewhere in the middle there was lunch. There always is. The paperwork on it is thin.

::prompt What was lunch and who found it?

The reason Porto Ercole is in this book at all: this is where Caravaggio stopped. July 1610, the pardon finally arranged — and the pardon and the paintings both on a felucca that had sailed without him. So he chased it. Up this coast, on foot, in that month, in that heat. He was thirty-eight.

He got exactly as far as Porto Ercole. On the 18th of July he was done. The felucca does not appear to have noticed.

::drop A man running out of coastline, the boat holding its line. Was he chasing the paintings or the pardon?

The town kept the grave. It is up there somewhere still, filed under a name nobody could spell. The paintings kept the water. That part of the story was not finished at Porto Ercole. Neither are we with it.
