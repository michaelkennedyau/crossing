Mdina bills itself as the Silent City, which is the sort of claim I normally like to audit, and I can report it held up. A walled town on a hill that stopped mattering around 1565 and has spent the centuries since declining to comment, streets a horse-width across, limestone the colour of the hour, doors with knockers above eye level for people who no longer answer them. Every other place on this trip had spent the day explaining itself at us, and here was a whole city with nothing to add, which was the point, so we walked it at the pace it asked for and did not add anything either.

::prompt What did Mdina sound like?

Back down at the Grand Harbour we crossed to the Three Cities by dghajsa, which is what happens when a gondola gets a proper job, and you sit at water level in a painted boat on a channel that has been moving somebody's cargo since before the Phoenicians invented the receipt. The Knights ran their fleet out of these creeks, the Royal Navy ran theirs, and now the same water carries tourists at the same unhurried angle, which as career changes go is not the worst.

::prompt What did the dghajsa boatman talk about?

::drop Maltese is the last living branch of Siculo-Arabic, and the most Catholic country in Europe says its prayers to Alla. What does a language keep that its speakers have agreed not to mention?

Then out along the coast to St Peter's Pool, which is not a pool so much as a shelf of pale rock the sea has cut into the shape of a decision. Flat stone, clear water, a drop of a few metres, and the place asks everyone who walks out onto the edge exactly one question. It is a very old question and the rock is completely neutral on it, having watched people answer it both ways for as long as there have been feet. We stood there a while with the water doing its colours below us.
